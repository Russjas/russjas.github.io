So how good is Tetracorder for core? - Part 2

hyperspectral
Tetracorder
An examination of Tetracorder performance on a benchmark hyperspectral dataset
Author

Published

October 20, 2026

Just like Part 1, this note draws heavily on the expertise of Thiele et al., not just their Benchmark Dataset, but also the methodology from their accompanying paper.

I ended Part 1 with some “Conclusions” or “Hypotheses”. After giving it some thought I have re-worked what I actually want to test:

  1. Tetracorder Group 2’s winner is the most abundant SWIR-active mineral.
  2. Tetracorder detects SWIR-active minerals reliably:
    1. even at low abundance, in spectrally neutral matrices;
    2. and the mineral it names is present, even when it isn’t the most abundant SWIR-active mineral.
  3. Tetracorder’s metrics track with mineral abundance.

I have decided not to test Group 1 winners here. Although the benchmark dataset does cover the same range as the Group 1 rules, my data does not, and I want to test Tetracorder for SWIR only instruments.

I will also only perform comparisons on the raw winners Tetracorder produces, not on my post-processing aggregations - these are still preliminary and will hopefully be informed by this work.

And one final caveat: all of the references to Tetracorder results in this note refer to results produced using python-tetracorder and not the authoritative release of Tetracorder. While I went to significant effort to reproduce the behaviour of the native code faithfully - this is not the native code.

Methodology

In the single pixel code above, Tetracorder decided it was clinochlore.fe.sc-cca-1, while the MLA shows that the pixel is dominated by quartz and feldspar. Tetracorder is never going to report quartz and feldspar - they are simply not active in the SWIR range. So, we need to compare Tetracorder with SWIR-active minerals only (as in Figure 2).

In the example pixel, the SWIR-active minerals in the MLA are chamosite and muscovite - so Tetracorder’s clinochlore is wrong. But is it completely wrong? Well, no, not completely. It has identified the mineral species incorrectly, but it has recognised that there is a chlorite there.

A hierarchy of apples to compare

So, how can you systematically compare a Tetracorder winner of the form clinochlore.fe.sc-cca-1 with MLA channels of the form Chamosite or Muscovite Mica? String matching, partial string matching or regex seem doomed to failure (as well as being no fun at all). We need to define a common vocabulary between the two datasets.

Mineral names come in levels, and neither dataset sticks to one level.

The spectral group is the level that SWIR spectroscopy actually sees. Tetracorder identifies an absorption, the features near 2.25 and 2.33 µm, then names the reference spectrum that best matches it. Getting the spectral group right means Tetracorder found the right kind of absorption. Getting the mineral group right means it matched the right family of reference spectra. Getting the species right means the fine detail of the feature, such as its exact position as Fe replaces Mg in a chlorite, also lined up.

The spectral group is not a neat layer above the mineral group. Most chlorites absorb at Fe/Mg-OH, but cookeite, a Li-Al chlorite, absorbs at Al-OH. So every species has exactly one mineral group and (for the purposes of this comparison) exactly one spectral group, and the two are treated as separate labels rather than a strict tree.

The MLA reports chamosite and clinochlore as species in some samples, but just “Chlorite” at another. Among the smectites it only reports nontronite. Tetracorder has species-level references, such as clinochlore.fe.sc-cca-1, alongside generic ones like chlorite, and mixtures such as muscovite+chlorite. So every MLA channel and every Tetracorder rule is mapped to all three levels, and a comparison can only be as fine as the coarser of the two sides. A generic “Chlorite” MLA channel can never produce a species match.

Each pixel gets the single finest level at which the two agree:

  1. Species: the same mineral.
  2. Group: a different species in the same mineral group.
  3. Spectral: a different mineral group with the same absorbing bond.
  4. Miss: none of the above.

Tetracorder can also return no winner at all and those are counted separately. A mixture rule counts as a match if any of its components matches.

Back to the example pixel: the MLA’s dominant SWIR-active mineral is chamosite, an Fe chlorite. Tetracorder called an Fe-rich clinochlore, which sits next to chamosite on the same Mg–Fe solid solution. That is a group match. Tetracorder didn’t name the right species, but it found the right absorption and the right mineral family.

So, to compare apples with apples, I need to define the mappings:

Tetracorder winning rule <–> Common species/group/spectral vocabulary <–> MLA channel

For our example pixel:

Tetracorder rule → common vocabulary:

rule species mineral group spectral group
clinochlore.fe.sc-cca-1 clinochlore chlorite Fe/Mg-OH

MLA channel → common vocabulary:

MLA channel species mineral group spectral group
Chamosite Chl chamosite chlorite Fe/Mg-OH

Constructing the lookups

Tetracorder has 213 rules that can produce a Group 2 result: 200 Group 2 rules, plus 13 case 6 rules that a Group 2 winner can hand over to (the calcite, epidote and chrysotile rules trigger case 6). Each rule lists the material, or materials, its reference spectrum represents, in RULE_LOOKUP_MATERIAL. Every material was assigned a species, mineral group and spectral group by hand. Where a rule’s material is only generic (chlorite, for example), the species is left blank, so that rule can never score a species match.

64 rules are mixtures of two to four materials, such as muscovite+chlorite or prehnite+.75chlorite. Mixtures are kept as one row per component, and a mixture counts as a match if any of its components matches.

The MLA has 121 distinct channel names, pooled over all 204 samples. Many are duplicates created by site naming: chamosite appears as “Chamosite”, “Chamosite Chl” and “FeChlorite”, depending on the site. Each channel was mapped to the same three levels, giving 14 species reported under more than one name. Where a channel only names a group, as “Chlorite” does at one site, its species is left blank. 44 of the 121 channels are SWIR-active (Al-OH, Fe/Mg-OH, CO3 or H2O). The other 77 are SWIR-inactive (quartz, feldspars, sulphides, oxides and so on) or aren’t minerals at all (Porosity, Unknown and Other).

Thiele et al. 2026 lookups

To score Tetracorder the same way the benchmark scores its models, both lookups also carry the paper’s group. These are the 22 mineral groups from the benchmark’s own data preparation notebook (Code/1_DataPrep.ipynb), whose thesaurus merges MLA channels into groups (chloritoid into chlorite, siderite into ankerite, and so on) and ignores Porosity, Unknown, Other and cassiterite. 72 of the 121 MLA channels fall into one of the 22 groups.

On the Tetracorder side, each rule component was assigned whichever of the 22 groups the paper would (presumably) file it under. White micas follow the paper’s split into muscovite, illite and lepidolite, siderite goes to ankerite, and all sulphates go to sulphate. 83 rules name nothing in the 22 groups (smectites, prehnite, serpentine, talc and so on). A pixel won by one of them predicts none of the 22, so in the paper’s scheme it can miss what’s there, but never score a hit or a false positive.

Using the lookups

In code, the two lookups do one job each. The Tetracorder lookup takes a winning rule and returns every species, mineral group and spectral group it names: one row for a single-material rule, several for a mixture. The MLA lookup takes a channel name and returns its single species, mineral group and spectral group. The cell below loads both, then puts the example pixel through them, so the two tables in the previous section are now computed rather than typed.

It also defines match_level, the rule from the previous section in code form: check species, then mineral group, then spectral group, and return the first level at which the winner and the MLA channel agree. A blank on the MLA side, such as the species of a generic “Chlorite” channel, can never match, so a comparison is never finer than its coarser side.

import pandas as pd

SWIR_ACTIVE = {"Al-OH", "Fe/Mg-OH", "CO3", "H2O"}                # spectral groups with a SWIR absorption
VOCAB = ["species", "group", "spectral_group"]                   # the three levels of the common vocabulary

rule_lookup = pd.read_csv("path/to/group2_crosswalk_long.csv", keep_default_na=False)
mla_lookup = pd.read_csv("path/to/mla_channels.csv", keep_default_na=False).set_index("mla_channel")

# Tetracorder side: the example pixel's winner, and everything it names (a mixture names several)
rule = winners[15, 23]
tet_sets = {level: set(rule_lookup.loc[rule_lookup["rule_id"] == rule, level]) - {""} for level in VOCAB}
print(rule, tet_sets)
#clinochlore.fe.sc-cca-1 {'species': {'clinochlore'}, 'group': {'chlorite'}, 'spectral_group': {'Fe/Mg-OH'}}

# MLA side: the example pixel's most abundant SWIR-active channel
pixel = pd.Series(mla[15, 23], index=mineral_channels).fillna(0)
active = pixel[mla_lookup.loc[pixel.index, "spectral_group"].isin(SWIR_ACTIVE)]
channel = active.idxmax()
truth = mla_lookup.loc[channel, VOCAB]
print(channel, round(active.max(), 3), truth.to_dict())
#Chamosite Chl 0.012 {'species': 'chamosite', 'group': 'chlorite', 'spectral_group': 'Fe/Mg-OH'}


# the finest level at which a winner and an MLA channel agree
def match_level(tet_sets, truth):
    for level, name in zip(VOCAB, ["species", "group", "spectral"]):
        if truth[level] != "" and truth[level] in tet_sets[level]:
            return name
    return "miss"


print(match_level(tet_sets, truth))
#group

Tetracorder runs

All runs were made with a single script, Tet_run_script.py, which writes each run to its own folder alongside a run_settings.json recording exactly how it was made.

Every run starts from the same FENIX reflectance cubes, with the first and last 10 bands dropped as noisy (from the pre-processing methodology of Thiele et al.), leaving 412–2448 nm. Pixels with any missing band are masked. Each run then varies one thing:

Run Savitzky–Golay window Order Enabled groups Bands used
1 11 2 1 and 2 full range
2 11 2 2 full range
3 5 1 2 full range
4 11 2 2 SWIR only, 970 nm and above

Smoothing (runs 2 and 3). In Part 1, I suggested that Savitzky–Golay smoothing with a window of 11 and a second-order polynomial suits Tetracorder best, while Thiele et al. used 5 and 1. Runs 2 and 3 differ only in this.

Group 1 (runs 1 and 2). I said at the start that comparisons are confined to Group 2, the group that matters for my own data. But some Group 1 rules act as NOT vetoes on Group 2 rules (calcite is vetoed by a Group 1 chlorite + muscovite reference, for example), and the benchmark’s FENIX data cover Group 1’s range. Run 1 enables Group 1 so its effect on Group 2 can be measured; Group 1’s own results aren’t compared.

True SWIR (run 4). The FENIX covers the VNIR and the SWIR, but a SWIR-only instrument doesn’t. Run 4 uses only the bands from the SWIR detector, which starts at 974 nm. The VNIR bands are passed to Tetracorder as invalid, so any feature or continuum window below 970 nm is out of range, and the smoothing is applied to the SWIR bands only, so the window never straddles the join between the two detectors. Group 2 rules that rely on a VNIR feature, such as siderite’s broad Fe²⁺ absorption, can’t be called. This is the run closest to scanning core with a SWIR camera.

All runs use Tetracorder’s default thresholds (mode="default"), room temperature (288–298 K), and case 6, which a Group 2 winner can hand over to. Group 0 normally competes in Group 2, and it is enabled, but every one of its rules is among the disabled materials below, so it never wins.

Disabled materials

Separately from whole groups, individual rules can be switched off by passing a list of rule ids to the run. The list for these runs comes from core_lookups.json, the lookup I use to map every Tetracorder rule onto a core-logging class. In that lookup, every rule whose reference isn’t a geological material is tagged non-geo: vegetation, liquid water, snow and ice, plastics, paints, fibreglass, hydrocarbons, cyanides and the World Trade Center dust and debris references. Portlandite (cement) and brucite are tagged non-geo too. Brucite is a real mineral, but on core it only ever fitted noise and water-band wiggles. None of these has any business winning on drill core, so all non-geo rules are disabled. While the full use of that particular lookup to classify core is preliminary, I am confident in the classifications of non-geo.

Five more rules are disabled by name. chalcedony and hydrated_basaltic_glass are broad, single-feature references with no NOT vetoes, so they fit almost any broad 2.2 µm or water absorption and win pixels they have no real claim to. They misbehave. azurite.ws316.g2, malachite.hs254.3b.g2 and nontronite.ng-1.a.g2 rely on VNIR features that a SWIR-only instrument can’t see, so they would be judged, poorly, on half their evidence. They are disabled in every run, so the runs differ only in the settings in the table.

A disabled rule is never evaluated, so any NOT veto that uses it never fires. This is native Tetracorder behaviour, and here it’s what I want. 44 Group 2 rules carry a veto sourced from a non-geo rule, almost all from white_pvc_pipe, dryveg.grass.long or vegetation1, including the whole chlorite, amphibole, serpentine and talc families. Those vetoes exist to stop Mg-OH rules winning on plastic and dry vegetation. Drill core carries neither, so on core any of those vetoes firing could only be a false veto.

Hypothesis testing

We have now defined what a Tetracorder run looks like, what the MLA data look like, and a common vocabulary to compare the two. All that remains is to actually compare them.

Each hypothesis is tested below on the example sample, Geyer bG16. (The full single sample example can be run from this script). The cells pick up the winners, fit, depth, mla and mineral_channels loaded earlier. Every comparison is made pixel by pixel. A pixel is used if the MLA has data there and it isn’t entirely porosity, unknown or other (those three together under 70%).

The same operations were then run over all 204 samples and every Tetracorder run, using this code, and those results are in the next section.

H1: is Group 2’s winner the most abundant SWIR-active mineral?

For each pixel, I take the most abundant SWIR-active MLA channel (the dominant mineral in the code block) and compare it with Tetracorder’s winner, using the lookups to put both into the common vocabulary. This is the most abundant channel, not the most abundant mineral group. Channels are what carry the species, so species matches need them. The cost is that a pixel with 8% chamosite and 7% clinochlore holds 15% chlorite, yet no single channel reaches the 10% threshold below, so the pixel isn’t counted. Each pixel gets the finest level at which the two agree:

  • Species: the winner names the same mineral species as the dominant mineral. For example, the MLA’s dominant mineral is clinochlore and Tetracorder’s winner is a clinochlore reference.
  • Group: the species differ, but both are in the same mineral group. For example, the MLA says chamosite and Tetracorder says clinochlore: both chlorites.
  • Spectral: the mineral groups differ, but both absorb through the same bond, so Tetracorder found the right kind of absorption but named the wrong mineral. For example, the MLA says amphibole and Tetracorder says chlorite: both Fe/Mg-OH.
  • Miss: the winner and the dominant mineral don’t agree at any level.
  • No call: Tetracorder has no winner for the pixel.

Each level only counts its own pixels, so a species match isn’t also counted as a group match. A comparison can be no finer than the coarser side: if the MLA channel only names a group (e.g. just “Chlorite”), a pixel can match at group level at best. A mixture rule matches if any of its components does. To keep this about minerals that are actually there in quantity, only pixels where the dominant mineral makes up at least 10% are counted.

That gives a breakdown of how Tetracorder does on each MLA mineral group, but it only looks one way. It shows how often Tetracorder gets chlorite pixels right, not how often it says chlorite when there isn’t any. For that, each mineral group is scored as a yes/no question on every pixel: did Tetracorder call chlorite, and is chlorite the dominant SWIR-active group? From that come:

  • Precision: when Tetracorder calls chlorite, how often is it right?
  • Recall: of the chlorite-dominated pixels, how many did Tetracorder call chlorite?
  • MCC (Matthews correlation coefficient): a single score from −1 to 1 that uses all four outcomes, with 0 meaning no better than chance. It’s the metric Thiele et al. use, and it isn’t flattered by rare classes the way accuracy is.

A mixture rule counts as a call for every mineral group it contains.
Each group is only scored in samples where the MLA reports it, so a site that doesn’t distinguish a mineral isn’t counted as a site where it’s absent.

Step by step, the cell below:

  1. Marks which MLA channels are SWIR-active, which are porosity, unknown or other, and which mineral group each channel belongs to.
  2. For each pixel, skips it if there’s no MLA data or it’s mostly porosity, unknown or other.
  3. Finds the dominant mineral: the most abundant SWIR-active channel, if it makes up at least 10%.
  4. Looks up everything Tetracorder’s winner names, at every level.
  5. Scores the winner against the dominant mineral at the finest level they agree, or records no call.
  6. Counts the match levels, then asks the yes/no question for each mineral group to get precision, recall and MCC.

#%% H1: Is Group 2's winner the most abundant SWIR-active mineral?
MIN_DOMINANT = 0.10                                               # dominant SWIR-active mineral must be at least 10%
EXCLUDE = ["Other", "Unknown", "Porosity"]

active_mask = mla_lookup.loc[mineral_channels, "spectral_group"].isin(SWIR_ACTIVE).to_numpy()
exclude_mask = np.isin(mineral_channels, EXCLUDE)
channel_group = mla_lookup.loc[mineral_channels, "group"].to_numpy()

rows = []
for x, y in np.ndindex(winners.shape):
    abundances = np.nan_to_num(mla[x, y])
    if abundances.sum() == 0 or abundances[exclude_mask].sum() > 0.7:
        continue                                        # no MLA data, or more than 70% Porosity, Unknown, Other

    # MLA side: the most abundant SWIR-active channel, and its mineral group
    active = np.where(active_mask, abundances, 0)
    dominant = mineral_channels[active.argmax()] if active.max() >= MIN_DOMINANT else ""

    # Tetracorder side: the winner and everything it names (a mixture names several)
    rule = winners[x, y]
    tet = rule_lookup[rule_lookup["rule_id"] == rule]
    tet_sets = {level: set(tet[level]) - {""} for level in VOCAB}

    # match level: the finest level at which the winner agrees with the dominant mineral
    if not dominant:
        match = ""
    elif not rule:
        match = "no call"
    else:
        match = match_level(tet_sets, mla_lookup.loc[dominant, VOCAB])

    rows.append({"winner": rule,
                 "dominant": dominant,
                 "dominant_group": channel_group[active.argmax()] if dominant else "",
                 "called_groups": tet_sets["group"], "match": match})

sample = pd.DataFrame(rows)

# match levels, for pixels with a dominant SWIR-active mineral
print(sample.loc[sample["dominant"] != "", "match"].value_counts())

#no call     349
#spectral      2
#Name: count, dtype: int64

# yes/no per mineral group, over every pixel: did Tetracorder call it, and is it the dominant SWIR-active group?
reported = sorted({channel_group[i] for i in np.flatnonzero(active_mask) if np.isfinite(mla[..., i]).any()})
for g in [g for g in reported if (sample["dominant_group"] == g).any()
          or sample["called_groups"].apply(lambda s: g in s).any()]:   # only groups this sample's MLA reports
    called = sample["called_groups"].apply(lambda s: g in s)
    truth = sample["dominant_group"] == g
    tp, fp = (called & truth).sum(), (called & ~truth).sum()
    fn, tn = (~called & truth).sum(), (~called & ~truth).sum()
    precision = tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else np.nan            # when Tetracorder calls g, how often is it right?
    recall = tp / (tp + fn) if tp + fn else np.nan               # of the g-dominated pixels, how many did it call g?
    denom = np.sqrt(float(tp + fp) * (tp + fn) * (tn + fp) * (tn + fn))
    mcc = (tp * tn - fp * fn) / denom if denom else np.nan       # -1 to 1, 0 = no better than chance
    print(f"{g:12s} n={tp + fn:4d}  precision={precision:.2f}  recall={recall:.2f}  MCC={mcc:.2f}")

The results for the test of this sample are dominated by biotite: 349 pixels where biotite is the most abundant SWIR-active mineral, and Tetracorder declined to call any of them. Biotite’s iron darkens the whole SWIR and flattens its OH features, and Tetracorder’s response to features too weak to match is to say nothing. That’s a failure mode we’ll see again.

Chlorite, white mica and smectite are more interesting. Neither is ever the dominant mineral here, yet Tetracorder called both, so every one of those calls counts as a false positive. The example pixel is one of them: Tetracorder spotted the chlorite, but at 1.2% this test can only score it as wrong. Whether that’s a false positive or a sensitive detection is what the next hypothesis tests.

H2: Tetracorder detects SWIR-active minerals reliably

H2a: does Tetracorder detect SWIR-active minerals at low abundance in a spectrally neutral matrix?

H1 only asks about the dominant SWIR-active mineral. H2a asks the opposite question: when a SWIR-active mineral is present in small amounts, and nothing else in the pixel absorbs in the SWIR, does Tetracorder find it?

“Nothing else absorbs” needs a definition. I call a pixel a spectrally neutral matrix for a mineral group if every other SWIR-active mineral in it adds up to less than 1%. The rest is quartz, feldspar and other minerals with no SWIR features. In those pixels, the mineral being tested is the only one the MLA reports with a SWIR feature in any quantity. That’s a mineralogical definition, not a spectral one: grain size, scattering and minerals the MLA doesn’t report can still shape the spectrum.

Abundance is split into bins (0–1%, 1–2.5%, 2.5–5%, 5–10%, 10–25% and over 25%), and for each bin I count how often Tetracorder named the mineral group: the detection rate. One extra bin, absent, holds the neutral pixels where the mineral group isn’t there at all. The detection rate in that bin is the false call rate: how often Tetracorder names a mineral that isn’t in the pixel. Without it, a high detection rate could just mean the mineral gets called everywhere.

H2b: is the mineral Tetracorder names actually there?

H1 scored a call as wrong whenever the named mineral wasn’t the dominant one, even if it may have been present at low abundance. H2a suggested that many of those calls are real detections, but on a very specific set of requirements. H2b turns the question round: for every call Tetracorder makes, is the mineral it names present in the pixel at all, whatever its abundance and whatever else is there?

Each call is checked against the MLA at the mineral group level. A mixture rule names several groups, and each is checked separately, so a muscovite + chlorite call asks both whether white mica is there and whether chlorite is there. A named group can be:

  • Present: the MLA has some of it in the pixel. I also report the stricter present at 1% or more, because the MLA’s smallest values are probably close to its noise.
  • Absent: the MLA reports that group in this sample, but none of it in this pixel. That’s an unconfirmed call: the MLA measures that group here and found none.
  • Not reported: the MLA doesn’t measure that group in this sample (alunite, jarosite or prehnite, for example). The MLA can neither confirm nor deny those calls, so they’re counted separately rather than as wrong.

All three are judged against the minerals the MLA reports, which makes this an operational truth, not an absolute one. A mineral group having a channel doesn’t guarantee the MLA picks up trace amounts of it, and smectite shows the asymmetry: the MLA’s only smectite is nontronite, while Tetracorder’s smectite references are mostly Al-OH smectites. Both map to the same group.

H3: do Tetracorder’s metrics track mineral abundance?

Tetracorder reports three numbers for each winner: the fit, how closely the shape of the absorption matches the reference spectrum; the depth, how strong the absorption is; and fit × depth. H3 asks whether any of them rises with how much of the mineral is actually there. If one does, it could stand in for abundance, which Tetracorder doesn’t otherwise report.

This test works at the mineral group level. For each call, the abundance it’s compared with is the MLA’s total for the group the winner names, added up over every channel in that group. A clinochlore winner on a pixel with 3% chamosite and 2% clinochlore is a chlorite call on 5% chlorite. Unlike H1, species-level matches aren’t counted separately; they’re counted along with the rest of their group, and the species detail is set aside. Depth responds to how much of the absorbing bond is there, and chamosite and clinochlore absorb through the same Fe/Mg-OH bond, just slightly shifted. Species abundances would also be fragile, because chlorite almost never matches at species level.

The test only uses correct calls: pixels where the winner names a single mineral group and the MLA says that group is present, at any abundance. A wrong call or no call has no meaningful abundance to compare against. Mixtures are left out, because it isn’t clear which component’s abundance the fit should track. Note this is a selected population: low-abundance pixels are the most likely to be missed altogether, and missed pixels never enter this test. So H3 asks whether fit and depth track abundance where Tetracorder has already recognised the mineral, not whether they could estimate abundance in any pixel.

The relationship is measured with Spearman correlation: a score from −1 to 1, where 1 means the fit (or depth) always rises as abundance rises, and 0 means they’re unrelated. It only asks whether one goes up with the other, not whether they follow a straight line. As a more readable version, the cell also prints the median fit, depth and fit × depth in each of the abundance bins from H2a.

Thiele et al. vocabulary tests

The benchmark models were trained and scored on the paper’s 22 mineral groups. For a direct comparison, I score Tetracorder the same way, reproducing their set-up from the benchmark notebooks:

  • Only the 61 test sections, the same ones the models never saw in training.
  • The same pixel mask, as far as the data allow: a 2 × 2 erosion at section edges (MLAStack’s clip=2), and pixels dropped where the 22 groups sum to less than 70%, with the rest renormalised to 100%.
  • Occurrence: a group is present in a pixel if its abundance is over 2.5%. This is the MCC in their published metrics.
  • Dominant phase: the most abundant of all 22 groups, quartz and feldspar included.

That leaves 52,722 test pixels, against the paper’s 50,146, about 5% more. The erosion is the same as the benchmark’s (the same function, with the same 2 × 2 element), so the difference isn’t at the section edges. It comes from two steps I couldn’t reproduce. The benchmark code intersects each section with its own mask band before eroding, and I couldn’t use that band (some loading issues). Its stacking code may also drop pixels without valid spectra in the other sensors (FX50, LWIR), which this comparison doesn’t use. So these scores are on a slightly larger set of pixels than the published ones, and I don’t know whether the extra pixels help or hurt Tetracorder.

These scores aren’t a SWIR-only test. Tetracorder can’t call quartz or feldspar, so whenever they dominate, its call can only be a false positive in the dominant-phase score. And any of its 83 rules that name nothing in the 22 groups can only score as a miss. That’s the price of using the benchmark’s own yardstick, and it’s why both sets of tests are reported.

Tetracorder can’t see any of them in the SWIR, so their recall is zero by construction, and the dominant-phase score has almost nothing left for Tetracorder to get right.

Second, the 2.5% threshold. H2b showed every one of Tetracorder’s chlorite calls landing on chlorite, at a median abundance of 2%. Under the paper’s occurrence rule, any chlorite under 2.5% is absent, so many of those correct detections count as false positives. Chlorite’s precision drops to 0.37 and its MCC to 0.14.

Third, the paper splits white mica into muscovite, illite and lepidolite. Tetracorder’s illite calls land on pixels where the MLA says muscovite, so they score as wrong twice: a false illite and a missed muscovite. Muscovite’s precision holds up (0.71), but its recall is just 0.07.

The remaining rows are minerals Tetracorder can’t see or doesn’t name. Fluorite and phosphate have no SWIR features, epidote is never called (as in H1), and the sulphate calls are the jarosite calls the MLA couldn’t confirm in H2b.

None of this makes the paper’s scheme wrong. It was built to score models that predict abundances of every mineral, including the ones Tetracorder can’t see. It was also built for models that use every wavelength range in the benchmark dataset, not just the SWIR.

Two sets of numbers answer different questions, and any comparison with the trained models has to be read with that in mind.

Discussion

What Tetracorder gets right

For white mica, chlorite, calcite or dolomite, Tetracorder is very good. When one of those wins, the MLA confirms its presence 95-99% of the time. Even when the pixel is dominated by SWIR-inactive minerals, Tetracorder often finds a few percent of chlorite or white mica: about half the time at 1–2.5% for chlorite, and two times in three for white mica.

Much of what H1 counted as wrong turns out to be exactly this: correct detections of minerals present at a few percent, in pixels where something else is more abundant. For alteration mapping, where the interesting mineral is often the minor one, that matters more than getting the dominant mineral right.

It also, mostly, fails safe. Tetracorder is reticent to name a mineral unless it is very confident; for the carbonates, no calls outnumber misses by about two to one. For an unsupervised method that is the desired behaviour.

And it does all of this on the SWIR alone, with no training, no site calibration, and default settings. Dropping the VNIR bands cost one mineral (siderite) and slightly improved calcite. Enabling Group 1 changed nothing. Changing the smoothing barely mattered. The results are robust to the settings I tried.

What it gets wrong

Three groups of minerals are a problem, each for a different reason.

Fe/Mg silicates other than chlorite. Amphibole and epidote are almost never named. Tetracorder finds their Fe/Mg-OH absorption and calls it chlorite, or prehnite + chlorite. This isn’t a failure to detect anything; it’s a failure to tell similar features apart, which comes down to the reference library and the rules. In a skarn, or any mafic-hosted system, Tetracorder will report chlorite where the rock is full of amphibole and epidote.

Biotite is invisible: no call in 84% of the pixels where it’s the dominant SWIR-active mineral, and never detected even at 5–25% in a clean matrix. Its iron darkens the whole SWIR and flattens the OH features. A biotite-altered porphyry would look, to Tetracorder, much like unaltered rock.

Dark carbonate. Calcite and dolomite are under-called: 22–26% no calls, and only 68–83% detection even in nearly pure carbonate. The worst samples are all from Stonepark, dark fine-grained limestone where the carbonate feature is likely to be shallow. I haven’t shown that the darkness is the cause, but it’s the obvious candidate. And this is a problem for me specifically, working in the Irish Carboniferous.

There are also calls that shouldn’t be trusted on their own. Smectite is confirmed by the MLA in fewer than 40% of its calls, prehnite in 10%, serpentine never. Some of that is the MLA’s blind spots rather than Tetracorder’s errors (the MLA reports no Al-OH smectite at all), but without independent evidence these calls are suspect.

Species is the wrong level for some minerals. Within a solid solution, such as chlorite or white mica, Tetracorder’s choice of reference doesn’t reliably match the MLA’s species. The mineral group is the honest level to report.

A lot of these failures are attributable to the specific use (abuse?) of Tetracorder in this case. Tetracorder spreads mineral identification across its groups, and I’ve only looked at Group 2. This test focussed on SWIR only references and rules and I either ignored or disabled the rules and references that cover the VNIR, which form a large part of the interconnected algorithm. Biotite, amphibole and epidote all have Fe²⁺ absorptions in the VNIR, which is Group 1’s territory, and I don’t use Group 1’s results at all. Scoring Group 2 alone asks Tetracorder to name these minerals without the part of the system designed to see them. I shouldn’t be surprised that when I cut pieces out of it, it doesn’t work as well.

Depth is a relative proxy for abundance

Tetracorder doesn’t report abundance, but its band depth tracks it. Within a mineral group, a deeper feature consistently means more mineral, with Spearman correlations of 0.45–0.66 and median depths that climb steadily across the abundance bins. Fit doesn’t: it measures how well the shape matches the reference, and once a mineral is recognised the shape matches well regardless of how much is there.

So depth can be used as a relative measure: this interval has more white mica than that one. It isn’t a calibration. The spread of abundance at any given depth is wide, and depths can’t be compared between minerals.

Against the trained models

On the benchmark’s own scoring, Tetracorder trails the models trained on this dataset for every mineral group. For the four it does best, it is 0.15–0.25 MCC behind. Tetracorder’s precision for those groups (0.76–0.90) is comparable with the models’. However, it lacks recall: it finds roughly half the occurrences the models find. The H2 results suggest why. A pixel that is mostly quartz and feldspar, with a few percent of mica, is a weak SWIR signal, and Tetracorder declines to call weak signals.

The comparison with the trained models isn’t like for like. I made it because the benchmark’s methodology was clearly laid out, designed for this very dataset, and the fairest yardstick available.

The trained models use the FENIX, FX50 and LWIR data, predict the abundance of every mineral including the ones Tetracorder can’t see, and were trained on these sites. Tetracorder uses the SWIR alone, has never seen this data, and wasn’t designed to estimate abundance. The models are better at the task they were built for. The question for core scanning is whether a method that needs no training data, and runs on any SWIR instrument, is good enough to be useful. On this evidence, for the common SWIR minerals, it is.

The limits of this test

The MLA is an operational truth. It’s the best mineralogy available at this scale (and public), but it has its own blind spots (no Al-OH smectite, no alunite, no pyrophyllite), its own detection limits, and its own registration to the hyperspectral pixels. Some of Tetracorder’s “false” calls may be real, and some of its “correct” ones may be lucky.

The lookups are my judgement. Every Tetracorder rule and every MLA channel was mapped to the common vocabulary by hand. The lookups are published so they can be checked, but different choices, for illite or chloritoid say, would move some numbers.

One benchmark, seven sites. Rock types and alteration styles the benchmark doesn’t cover may (will!) behave differently, and the per-site differences (Stonepark’s carbonates, for one) show how much the rock matters.

This is python-tetracorder. These results come from my Python port of Tetracorder, run with its default thresholds. The port follows the native code closely, but it is not the native code, and the default thresholds were not designed for core.

What does this mean for core scanning?

For core scanning, the practical value of a mineral classification depends on the question being asked.

If the objective is a quantitative mineralogical model of every pixel, including quartz, feldspar and minerals without diagnostic SWIR features, Tetracorder is not the right tool by itself. The benchmark’s trained models, and the underlying methodology, are demonstrably better suited to that task.

If the objective is to identify the distribution of spectrally active alteration minerals, the results are considerably more favourable. White mica and chlorite can be detected at relatively low abundance, and their positive classifications generally correspond to minerals reported by the MLA. That is useful information even when those minerals represent only a small fraction of the rock.

The principal caution is that a missing classification is not evidence that the mineral is absent. Detection sensitivity differs strongly between minerals, and even abundant carbonates may receive no call. Equally, positive identifications of less reliable groups need more scrutiny than those of white mica, chlorite or the carbonates.

There are practical advantages to the rule-based approach. The rules are inspectable, the reference materials are identifiable, and the system does not require a training dataset representative of every new lithology or geological setting. For surveys and other organisations processing large volumes of varied core, those properties have value. They do not guarantee that a classification will transfer correctly to unfamiliar geology, but they make its assumptions available for examination and its failures open to investigation.

There is also scope for improvement. The experiments identify particular minerals and rules that deserve attention, rather than requiring the entire system to be reconsidered. A core-specific configuration, improved reference selection or more discriminating rules could address some of the observed confusions. Whether those changes actually improve performance would, of course, need to be tested rather than assumed.

What could be improved

Most of the failures can be traced to specific rules, which means they can be fixed in a transparent way. Siderite’s reliance on a 1 µm feature, the vetoes on the calcite rules, and the reference spectra for chlorite, amphibole and epidote are all candidates. A core-specific configuration, improved reference selection or more discriminating rules could address some of the observed confusions. If, and how, those changes actually improve performance would, of course, need to be tested rather than assumed.

Tuning the rules on this benchmark and then testing them on the same benchmark would no longer be testing Tetracorder. It would be fitting it. That needs a held-out test set, and is work for another day.

Conclusion

So how good is Tetracorder for core? Good enough. Probably. For some things.
